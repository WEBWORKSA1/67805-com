How to build an AI stack for a small business

The layers, the order and the budget, with three example stacks you can copy.

By the 67805.com editorial teamUpdated October 27, 20269 min read

Most small businesses do not have an AI problem. They have a tool-pile problem: five subscriptions bought in a burst of enthusiasm, two used once, none connected to the way work actually flows. An AI stack fixes that. It is a short list of tools, one per job, chosen in a deliberate order and connected so information moves without anyone copying and pasting.

This guide walks through the layers of a practical stack, the order to add them in, what a sensible budget looks like, and three example stacks for common business types.

Think in layers, not apps

A stack is easier to plan when you think of it as layers, each with a single job. Apps are interchangeable; layers are not. If you know which layer you are filling, you can swap one app for another later without rebuilding everything.

LayerJob it doesTypical tools
Base: assistantDaily drafting, summarizing, thinking through problemsClaude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot
Front doorAnswers customer questions and captures leadsWebsite chat and help-desk tools such as Tidio, Intercom or Zendesk
MemoryHolds customer, deal and project informationA CRM or shared workspace such as HubSpot or Notion
OutputCreates content, visuals, video and documentsCanva, Descript, Gamma and similar
GlueMoves data between apps and runs workflowsZapier, Make, n8n
Back officeBookkeeping, receipts, invoicing, contractsQuickBooks, Xero, Dext, e-signature tools

You do not need every layer on day one. A solo consultant might run on the base layer plus a meeting recorder for a year. A 20-person e-commerce brand will want the front door and glue layers early, because that is where the repetitive hours are.

The order matters more than the brand

Teams that succeed with AI tend to add layers in roughly this order.

  1. The assistant first. It costs little, everyone can use it, and it builds the habit of handing first drafts to AI. Give the team a shared set of prompts from day one so results are consistent. Our prompt library is a starting point.
  2. Then the biggest bottleneck. Look at where the most repetitive hours go. For service businesses that is usually customer questions and scheduling. For agencies it is reporting and content. For stores it is product content and support.
  3. Then the glue. Once two or three tools are in daily use, connect them. The glue layer is where AI moves from saving minutes to saving hours, because it removes the hand-offs between apps.
  4. Back office last. Finance automation pays well but carries more risk if something goes wrong, so add it once the team trusts AI output and has review habits.

How to choose a tool inside each layer

For each layer, shortlist two tools and test them for two weeks on real work. Judge them on five questions:

  • Does it fit how we already work? A tool that lives inside an app you use daily beats a better tool in a separate tab.
  • Can a non-technical person set it up? If only one person can maintain it, you have created a single point of failure.
  • What happens to our data? Check whether your inputs are used for training, where data is stored and whether there is a business plan with stronger terms.
  • Does it connect to the glue layer? A tool with no integrations becomes an island.
  • What does it cost at our size in a year? Per-seat pricing grows fast. Multiply by your headcount twelve months from now.

What should an AI stack cost?

For a small business, a useful rule is to keep AI software under 10% of the value of the time it saves. If the stack saves 40 hours a month and an hour is worth $40, the time is worth $1,600 a month, so up to $160 in subscriptions is easy to justify. Run your own numbers in the ROI calculator.

Budget for setup time too. Expect 10 to 30 hours of setup and training for the first two layers. That time is the real investment, and it is the main reason stacks fail when nobody owns it.

Three example stacks

Local service business (plumber, salon, clinic)

Assistant for quotes and replies; a website chat tool that answers hours, prices and availability and books appointments; a review-reply prompt set; accounting software with automatic receipt capture. Biggest win: fewer phone interruptions and faster quotes.

Online store

Assistant for product descriptions; an image tool for product photos and backgrounds; a support chatbot grounded in shipping and returns policies; an automation that tags orders, updates the spreadsheet and sends review requests. Biggest win: support tickets answered instantly around the clock.

Agency or consultancy

Assistant for drafts and research; a meeting recorder that sends summaries and action items to the CRM; a presentation tool for proposals; an automation layer for client reporting. Biggest win: hours of reporting and note-taking returned to billable work.

Common mistakes

  • Buying before mapping. If you cannot name the task and the hours it takes, you cannot tell whether a tool worked.
  • Rolling out to everyone at once. Start with one team and one workflow, then copy what works.
  • No owner. Someone needs a few hours a week to maintain prompts, automations and access.
  • No review step. Anything customer-facing or financial needs a human check until the error rate is known.

Frequently asked questions

What is the first AI tool a small business should buy?

A general AI assistant on a business plan. It is inexpensive, everyone can use it, and it builds habits that make every later tool more useful.

How many AI tools does a small business need?

Usually three to five: an assistant, one or two tools for the biggest bottleneck, an automation tool to connect them, and later a back-office tool.
