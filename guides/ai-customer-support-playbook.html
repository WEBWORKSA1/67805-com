The AI customer support playbook for small businesses

Automate the repeat questions, keep humans on the hard ones, and protect your reputation while you do it.

By the 67805.com editorial teamUpdated October 27, 20269 min read

Customer support is usually the single biggest source of repetitive work in a small business. The same twenty questions arrive every week: hours, prices, shipping times, returns, how to reset a password. AI handles those well. The goal is not to remove humans from support. It is to free them for the conversations where a human makes the difference.

Step 1: Sort your tickets

Export or skim the last 100 to 200 customer messages and sort them into three buckets:

  • Answerable from facts: hours, prices, policies, order status. AI can answer these on its own.
  • Needs judgment: refunds outside policy, complaints, custom quotes. AI can draft; a human decides.
  • Needs a human: upset customers, legal or safety issues, high-value accounts. Route straight to a person.

In most small businesses, the first bucket is 40 to 70 percent of volume. That is your automation target.

Step 2: Write the knowledge base the AI will use

An AI support agent is only as good as the information you give it. Collect your policies, prices, product details and common answers into clear, short articles. Write them in the words customers use, not internal jargon. The prompt library includes a prompt that turns your existing notes into question-and-answer pairs.

Rule of thumb: if a new employee could not answer the question from your knowledge base, the AI cannot either.

Step 3: Choose where the AI works

SetupBest forRisk
Suggested replies for agentsTeams starting out, sensitive industriesLow: a human sends every reply
Website chatbot for factual questionsStores and local services with high volumeMedium: needs good knowledge base and hand-off
Fully automated email repliesMature teams with tested contentHigher: monitor closely

Start with suggested replies or a chatbot limited to factual questions. Expand only when you have a few weeks of data showing answers are accurate.

Step 4: Design the hand-off

The worst support experience is a bot that will not let you reach a person. Make sure that:

  • Customers can ask for a human at any point and get one.
  • The human sees the full conversation, so the customer does not repeat themselves.
  • The bot tells customers when to expect a human reply.
  • Certain words, such as refund, cancel, complaint or lawyer, route to a person automatically.

Step 5: Set guardrails

  • The bot must never promise refunds, discounts or delivery dates it cannot verify.
  • It must say it does not know rather than guess.
  • It must not ask for passwords or full card numbers.
  • It should state clearly that the customer is talking with an automated assistant.

Step 6: Measure weekly

Track the share of conversations resolved without a human, customer satisfaction on bot conversations versus human ones, first response time, and the questions the bot failed to answer. That last list is gold: each failure is a missing knowledge-base article.

What results to expect

A well-set-up chatbot usually resolves a meaningful share of factual questions in the first month and improves as the knowledge base fills in. First response times drop from hours to seconds for those questions. Human agents spend their time on the complex cases, which tends to improve satisfaction scores for those conversations too.

Frequently asked questions

Will customers be annoyed by a chatbot?

They are annoyed by bots that trap them or give wrong answers. A bot that answers factual questions instantly and hands off quickly is usually welcomed.

Do I need to tell customers they are talking to AI?

It is good practice everywhere and required by law in some places. Label the assistant clearly.
