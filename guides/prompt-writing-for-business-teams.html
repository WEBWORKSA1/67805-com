Prompt writing for business teams: a practical guide

A simple four-part pattern, the constraints that remove filler, and how to build a shared prompt library your team will use.

By the 67805.com editorial teamUpdated October 27, 20268 min read

The quality gap between people who get great results from AI and people who give up is rarely the tool. It is the prompt. The good news: writing effective prompts is a learnable business skill, closer to writing a clear brief for a freelancer than to programming.

The four-part pattern

  1. Role: who the AI should act as. “You are an experienced B2B copywriter.”
  2. Context: who you are, who the audience is, what good looks like. Paste source material here.
  3. Task: one clear job. “Write three subject lines for this email.”
  4. Format: length, structure, tone. “Under 60 characters each, as a numbered list.”

Most weak prompts skip context. The AI fills the gap with generic assumptions, and the output reads generic.

Constraints that remove filler

  • Length limits. “Under 120 words” forces focus.
  • Banned words. List the clichés you never want to see.
  • Source limits. “Use only the facts below” reduces invented details.
  • Uncertainty flags. “If you are unsure, say so” makes errors visible.
  • Options. “Give three versions” is faster than editing one.

Iterate like an editor

Treat the first answer as a draft. Give specific feedback: “Shorter. More direct. Replace the second paragraph with a customer example.” Two or three rounds usually beat starting over. When you find a prompt that works, save it.

Build a shared prompt library

A team prompt library turns individual tricks into consistent results. Keep it simple:

Examples: before and after

Weak

“Write a post about our new service.”

Strong

“You are a social media manager for a family-owned HVAC company in a mid-size city. Our audience is homeowners aged 35–65. We just launched same-day furnace repair with a 2-hour arrival window. Write three Facebook posts under 80 words each. Lead with the customer’s problem, not our name. No exclamation marks. End each with a question that invites comments.”

Common mistakes

  • Asking for everything in one prompt. Split big jobs into steps.
  • Accepting the first answer.
  • Pasting sensitive data into tools without approved data terms.
  • Not saving what works.
