How to measure AI ROI without fooling yourself

Baselines, hidden costs and the one metric that tells you whether an AI tool is paying off.

By the 67805.com editorial teamUpdated October 27, 20268 min read

Vendors quote dramatic productivity numbers. Your accountant wants to know whether the subscription is worth renewing. The honest answer sits between those two and you can find it with a simple method: a baseline, a pilot, and a comparison that counts every cost.

Step 1: Set a baseline before you change anything

Pick one workflow, for example replying to customer emails, writing weekly social posts or reconciling receipts. For one normal week, record:

  • How many times the task happens
  • How long each takes, on average
  • Who does it and their loaded hourly cost (wage plus taxes and benefits)
  • Quality signals: errors, rework, customer complaints, response time

A shared spreadsheet is enough. Without this baseline, every result afterwards is a guess.

Step 2: Run a time-boxed pilot

Use the AI tool on the same workflow for two to four weeks. Keep recording the same numbers. Expect the first week to be slower while people learn; judge the tool on weeks two to four.

Step 3: Count every cost

The subscription is usually the smallest cost. Include:

CostHow to estimate
SubscriptionsSeats × monthly price, at the headcount you will have in a year
SetupHours to configure, connect and write prompts × hourly cost
TrainingHours per person to learn the tool
Review timeMinutes spent checking AI output, per task
MaintenanceHours per month to fix broken automations and update prompts

Step 4: Do the math

The core formula is simple:

Monthly net savings = (hours saved per month × loaded hourly cost) − monthly tool costs − monthly review and maintenance time.
Payback period = one-time setup cost ÷ monthly net savings.

Example: a support assistant cuts average reply time from 6 minutes to 2 minutes on 600 emails a month. That is 40 hours saved. At $30 an hour, $1,200 of time. Subtract $150 in subscriptions and 6 hours of review ($180), and the net is $870 a month. With 15 hours of setup ($450), payback is just over two weeks.

The metric that matters: where did the hours go?

Saved time only becomes money when it goes somewhere useful: more sales calls, more billable work, less overtime, or a hire you can delay. If the saved time disappears into a slightly calmer week, that has value, but count it at a lower rate. Ask each person in the pilot what they did with the reclaimed time.

Signals that the numbers are wrong

  • Savings with no change in output. If the team says it is faster but nothing more gets done, re-check the baseline.
  • Quality slipped. Rising complaints or rework can wipe out the time saved.
  • Only one enthusiast uses it. Adoption below half the team usually means the tool does not fit the workflow.

Revenue gains are upside

Faster replies can lift conversion, and better content can bring more traffic. These gains are real but hard to attribute. Keep them out of the core ROI number and report them separately, so the decision rests on what you can measure.

When to cancel

If after a month the net savings are negative, or adoption is under half the intended users, either fix the workflow or cancel. Sunk setup time is not a reason to keep paying.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good ROI for AI tools?

For workflow tools in a small business, aim for payback within three months and net savings at least three times the subscription cost.

How long should an AI pilot run?

Two to four weeks on one workflow. Ignore the first week, when people are still learning.
